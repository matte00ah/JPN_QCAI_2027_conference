\paragraph{Crater detection}
Deep learning is the standard tool for automatic crater cataloguing. DeepMoon~\cite{silburt2019deepmoon} trains a
convolutional network on lunar digital elevation maps, recovers 92\,\% of the craters of a human-made test catalogue
and transfers to Mercury. Object detectors of the YOLO family target the small craters that dominate the counts,
e.g.\ YOLO-Crater~\cite{mu2023yolocrater}. Closer to our setting, several works combine optical images and elevation
data: Tewari et~al.~\cite{tewari2020automated} stack optical, elevation and slope maps as the input of a Mask R-CNN,
and Lin et~al.~\cite{lin2024adsnet} fuse the two modalities in a dual-branch network with attention, reporting a gain
over single-modality inputs. These detectors localise craters (centre and radius) and are trained end to end and
evaluated against catalogues over large areas. Our task is different by design: frozen foundation-model backbones,
a classification of 1.6\,km sub-tiles of a 100\,m grid (crater centre with $1 \le D < 8$\,km or not) instead of a localisation, and a
second stage whose training set is limited to at most 2{,}000 samples by the cost of the kernel. We do not compete
with these detectors; we take their observation that optical and elevation data are complementary as the setting of
a controlled comparison between quantum and classical kernels on identical inputs.

% --- Part 2: Discussion, limits (2-3 sentences) ---
\paragraph{Scope}
This study is a controlled testbed, not a competitive detector: one equatorial region (30$^\circ$S--30$^\circ$N,
0--90$^\circ$E), frozen backbones, proxy pre-processing, sub-tile labels from a single catalogue and small stage-2
samples. Its figures of merit are therefore not comparable with those of the detectors above, which differ in task,
metric, catalogue, region and resolution; what the study measures is the relative behaviour of quantum and classical
kernels under the same conditions.
